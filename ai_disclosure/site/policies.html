Each project, its own rules

Open Source projects do not agree on AI-assisted contributions. Some accept them, some forbid them, and those that ask for a declaration ask for it in different ways. This page compares the rules we read, and drafts a short statement that a project could make about itself.

The practice this site follows is one of them. It is described on the good practice page.

What the table shows:

  • Those who accept AI code agree on the person. Someone must understand the change, be able to explain it, and answer for it. This is the common ground.
  • An agent does not act alone. NumPy, pip, Mesa and the Linux kernel each forbid a tool that submits by itself. The other projects do not speak of it.
  • Several keep the conversation human. NumPy, pandas, Mesa and Kubernetes do not want a model to write the descriptions, the comments or the answers to a review.
  • There is no shared way to declare. Three trailer names are in use, and the same trailer is required by one project and forbidden by another. Some projects name the model, one forbids it, and others ask for a sentence in the pull request.

The last point is the weak one for everybody. A contributor has to learn a different rule in each project, and no tool can read all of them. Agreeing on one line would help more than any of these rules taken alone.

For more projects, see the community list open-source-ai-contribution-policies, which follows more than a hundred of them. It is an index: check each rule on the project's own page, as some lines are out of date.

The trailer has other names

Projects do not agree on the name of the line. Among those we read:

  • Assisted-by is required by Mesa and by Fedify. Both keep Co-Authored-By for people: in their words, it is "reserved for human co-authors".
  • Generated-by is what Mesa asks for when almost all the code was generated, and one of the two forms the Apache Software Foundation recommends. The other one is Co-authored-by.
  • No trailer at all for pip and Kubernetes, which do not allow a model to be listed as a co-author.

The practice of this site keeps Co-Authored-By for a practical reason: the forges already show it, and several tools already write it without being asked. The objection is fair, though. A model is not an author like a person is, and git has no better place for it yet.

The name of the trailer matters less than three things, which hold whatever a project chooses: the line is in the commit, a script can read it, and it names the model and its version.

The pages of this site therefore read the three names. An Assisted-by or a Generated-by line counts when it names a model the tool recognises, for instance Assisted-by: Claude:claude-opus-5-5. A line that names a person, or a tool that is not an AI, is left unread.

Judged on merit: the OCaml guidelines

The practice of this site rests on one idea: a contribution is judged on what it is, and declaring AI use never counts against it. Our reference for this idea is the OCaml compiler, which states it better than we do. Its guidelines for AI-assisted contributions, in the repository since November 2025, are six short rules, and the first one is:

We strive to evaluate contributions based on their merit, not their authors.

A change is judged on what it is: correct or not, understood or not, worth a reviewer's time or not. Who or what wrote it is not the test. So there is no ban and no separate track: a good change written with a model is accepted, and a bad one is refused because it is bad.

Merit alone would be an empty word. The other rules are what make it work:

  • The contributor answers for all of it. "We ask that you have either written, or read and reviewed yourself, every part of your contribution. You are responsible for its quality."
  • AI use is disclosed, like any other help. A significant AI-generated part "must be disclosed, stating which tool was used and for what", because reviewing it "can require a different approach". The same rule adds that an acknowledgement is owed "even if another human contributor did some of the work".
  • The reviewer's time is what is protected. It "is a scarce resource", and contributions, "whether human- or AI-authored", should conserve it.
  • There is a floor. Never acceptable: "low-effort productions of code that contributors do not understand", plagiarised works, hallucinated security reports.

What we take from it is the sentence that introduces the rules: they "also apply, or have a direct counterpart, with entirely human-produced content". Nothing is asked of a person who uses AI that is not asked of everyone. Declaring is then information for the review, never a verdict on the author, and that is what lets a project ask for it without shaming anyone.

Judging on merit does not mean that the way a change was made is of no interest. It matters to the person who reviews it, and to anyone who later asks where the code came from. It means that the origin is said, and then not held against the author.

The OCaml experiment

In April 2026, Anil Madhavapeddy proposed a voluntary disclosure for OCaml code, built on an earlier draft of the W3C report, which then had four values (it separated assisted from generated). It is the most precise scheme we know of:

  • In the code, not in the history. The declaration is written in the package file, at the top of a module, or on a single function, and the nearest one wins.
  • The model by its API identifier, "not a marketing name", with the provider.
  • The agent writes it, the reviewer adjusts it. A person who has reviewed a function and taken ownership of it may lower the mark or remove it: the marks exist "to direct review attention, not to permanently brand code".

The last point is where it differs from a trailer. A mark in the code describes the code as it stands, and answers "what still needs a review?". A trailer describes what happened, and stays true after the review. A project can want both.

The proposal was discussed without being adopted, and in September 2026 its author removed the agent instructions that wrote the marks, without giving a reason. We read it as a sign of what a declaration costs: a mark on every function has to be kept true at every change, a line at the end of a commit is written once.

Where does a project stand? draft

Before contributing, people want to know what a project accepts. Most projects do not say, and those that do each say it their own way. We are drafting a short statement that a project could make about itself. It has two answers.

1. What the project accepts

The statement uses the three values of the shared vocabulary, with one word in front: accepts. A project names the furthest value it takes.

StatementWhat the project saysAmong those we read
accepts human-onlyOnly contributions written without AI.SourceHut, for the projects it hosts.
accepts ai-assistedAI may take part, lightly or largely, when a person has read, understood and tested the change, and answers for it.CPython, NumPy, pandas, pip, OCaml, Mesa, Fedify, the Apache Software Foundation, Kubernetes, the Linux kernel.
accepts ai-autonomousAlso contributions that no person has reviewed, for example from an agent the project runs or invites.No project. One of the ready-made texts of ai-policy.dev allows it.
unspecifiedThe project states nothing. This is not a position, and it is the most common case today.Several of the projects followed here, as far as we could find.

The word accepts is not decoration. ai-assisted alone is a fact about one contribution. "Accepts human-only" is a rule a project sets for what it takes from now on: it does not claim that no AI was ever used in the project, which nobody could show.

2. How AI use is declared

The same statement hides opposite rules: NumPy rejects a pull request that does not disclose, and pip closes one that names a model as co-author. So the statement needs a second answer:

  • Whether: required, recommended, not asked, or forbidden.
  • Where: in the commit, with the name of the trailer; in the pull request; or both.
  • What: that a tool was used, which tool, or the model and its version.

For the practice of this site, the statement reads: accepts ai-assisted; declared in every commit with Co-Authored-By naming the model and its version, and in the pull request.

Notes on the draft

  • The statement is what a project says, in its own files. The charts of this site show what its commits declare. The two can differ, and seeing both is the point.
  • No statement is the right one. A project that refuses AI and a project that accepts it both help contributors by saying so.
  • Choosing one is the project's freedom. The freedoms of free software protect the people who run, study, change and share a program. They do not say how a contribution has to be made, and they give no contributor a right to have their way of working accepted. A project that accepts human-only and a project that accepts ai-assisted use the same freedom: to decide what they take. What a contributor keeps, in every case, is the freedom to fork.
  • The values are the W3C group's, the statements are ours. None of the projects in the last column describes itself this way. Placing real projects was the test of the draft, and it changed it.
  • Nothing says "largely generated". An earlier draft of ours had a label for it. No project we read draws that line as a rule of acceptance, and the W3C report dropped it too: what counts is whether a person reviewed the change, not how much of it a model wrote. A project that wants to say more can do it in the declaration, as Mesa does with two trailers.
  • Nothing says "we would rather not". An earlier draft of ours had a label for it. Among the rules we read, none says it without forbidding. It may be a position that projects hold and do not write down.
  • Ready-made policies tested the draft again. Three of the four texts of ai-policy.dev are easy to place. "AI Disallowed" accepts human-only. "Human Voice" accepts ai-assisted. "AI Allowed" accepts ai-autonomous: a contribution is welcome "whether it was created by a human or an agent". The fourth, "Human Responsible", does not fit. Everything submitted must be written by the contributor, yet "what happens on your machine is not policed, will not be asked about". It stands between human-only and ai-assisted, and the statement has no word for it.
  • Declaring can be forbidden. The same text asks contributors not to add notes about AI tools. The second answer had three values, and now has a fourth.
  • Limits of size are not in it. Kubernetes accepts AI help and refuses large generated pull requests. The statement keeps the first and loses the second.
  • A forge can decide for its projects. SourceHut's rule applies to the original content of every project it hosts. A project there has a statement it did not choose, so there are two levels: the project, and the place where it lives.

This is a draft: the wording may change.

A project can already make its statement in a file. This site reads ai-policy.yaml, a format proposed by others, and shows what it says on the page of the project, beside what its commits declare.

Related work

The Software Freedom Conservancy has published recommendations on LLM-backed generative AI for free and open source software, written with a group of volunteers. Several of them meet what is described on the good practice page:

  • support those who reject these systems, and let every contributor decide for themselves;
  • do not shun contributors who use them;
  • review and understand a contribution before submitting it;
  • disclose which system was used and its version, in a machine-readable form in the commit log;
  • submit contributions that no human has vetted only where a project explicitly asks for them.

Their recommendations go further on subjects this site does not cover, such as copyright and licensing, keeping a record of prompts, and the use of proprietary tools. The Co-Authored-By trailer is one concrete way to do the disclosure they call for.

On the same question, from other angles:

  • the W3C community group report on AI Content Disclosure for HTML, whose three values are used on this site;
  • the disclosure proposal for OCaml, which declares AI use in the code and in the package rather than in the commit;
  • the rules of other projects, and the forge that chose to prohibit.

A file that tools can read. ai-policy.yaml is a proposal for a small file, kept at the root of a repository, in which a project declares what it accepts: whether AI may produce a contribution, whether its use must be declared and how, whether a person must have reviewed the change, and whether an agent may submit on its own. It describes itself as an early proposal whose design is not settled. It covers the two answers of the draft statement of this site, so we read it instead of proposing another file: when a project has one, its page shows what the file says. For our own use it lacks two things, which the project files of this site keep for now: the date from which a rule applies, and the wording of the boxes of a pull request template. Both were reported to the proposal.

Ready-made policies exist too. ai-policy.dev is a community collection of four texts, from "AI Allowed" to "AI Disallowed", that a project can copy into an AI_POLICY.md file. None of the four asks for a declaration. The most open one says that "AI usage does not need to be declared". Another one, "Human Responsible", forbids it: "a submission that discloses AI usage may be closed automatically". A text that accepts AI and asks that it be declared is not in the collection; it is the practice described on the good practice page.

European law meets the subject from another side. Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act asks the providers of AI systems to mark what their systems generate "in a machine-readable format". The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, published in June 2026, says how. Both speak to those who provide the systems and to those who publish for the public, not to a project or its contributors, and neither mentions source code.

Two of their ideas are also ours. The Code encourages providers to record the name of the AI system, and they "may also include the underlying AI model identifier and version number". And a published text needs no label when a person has reviewed it and "holds editorial responsibility" for it.
